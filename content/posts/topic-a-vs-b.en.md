Judge enough science fairs and a pattern shows up: plenty of topics have real potential but lose out because the question was asked too loosely. So let's do this differently — no answers up front. You be the judge.

![Headphones on a flower — does sound really affect plants?](/blog/music-plant-2.jpg)

## Example 1: the research question

Student A and Student B both want to study "the effect of sound on plants." Here's how each of them wrote the question:

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student A:</strong> "Does music affect plant growth?"</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student B:</strong> "The effect of different sound frequencies (50 Hz, 500 Hz, silent control) on the 7-day height of mung bean seedlings"</div>

**If you were the judge, out of 5, what would you give each?** Put a number in your head first, then open this up.

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see how a judge scores these</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Student A · about 1 point.</strong> The question is too big and too vague: which music? Affecting what — height, leaf count? Measured how? No variable, no control. A judge can tell from the title alone that this will be hard to do rigorously.</p>
<p><strong>Student B · about 5 points.</strong> Clear variable (frequency), a control group (silence), a measurable outcome (7-day height). Small enough to measure precisely; specific enough to have a comparison.</p>
<p><strong>The key difference:</strong> both students are studying the same thing. B simply swapped "does it affect" for "which variable, on which measurable outcome, by how much." That's what judges are after.</p>

## Example 2: the conclusion

Now assume both students ran the same experiment and got the same data. Here's how each wrote the conclusion:

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student A:</strong> "This study proves that 500 Hz sound promotes plant growth and could be used in agriculture."</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student B:</strong> "Under these experimental conditions, the 500 Hz group averaged 12% taller at day 7 (n = 15). However, the sample size was limited and the experiment was not repeated across batches, so further testing is needed."</div>

**Same drill: who gets the higher score?**

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see how a judge scores these</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Student A · about 2 points.</strong> A small sample, run once — and yet words like "proves" and "could be used in agriculture." The conclusion is overstated, and a judge will read it as careless.</p>
<p><strong>Student B · about 5 points.</strong> The conclusion stays tied to the data, and names the limits without being asked.</p>
<p><strong>The key difference:</strong> many students think admitting a weakness costs points, so they push the conclusion further. To a judge, the student who states the limits looks more mature and more trustworthy.</p>
</div>
</details>

## One line to take away

Notice how obvious it becomes once you're the one holding the scoring sheet? Student B is ahead on two things: **a sharper question and a more honest claim** —

- Question: from "does it affect" to "which variable → which measurable outcome → by how much"
- Conclusion: from "I proved it" to "under these conditions the data show…, though the limits are…"

Neither shift needs pricier equipment or a flashier topic. What it needs is someone sitting alongside your child, helping them ask it clearly and say it plainly.

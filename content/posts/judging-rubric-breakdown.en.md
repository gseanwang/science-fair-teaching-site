Most students assume judges score on impression. In reality, a judge usually has a **rubric** in hand, and every row spells out exactly what counts as "excellent" and what counts as "needs improvement."

Learn to read that sheet and you're essentially holding the answer key. Below I use the **publicly available 2024–2025 preliminary-review rubric from the Junior Science and Humanities Symposium (JSHS)** — the official document they hand to participants — and walk through it row by row, with a few examples so you can feel the difference between a strong and a weak version of the same thing.

## First: how does the scoring work?

A judge scores **every row** from 0 to 5, in three bands:

<div class="cx-scale">
<span class="cx-scale-item cx-scale-good">✅ Excellent 4–5</span>
<span class="cx-scale-item cx-scale-mid">🟡 Acceptable 2–3</span>
<span class="cx-scale-item cx-scale-warn">⚠️ Needs work 0–1</span>
</div>

Add the rows together and you have the project's total — the more rows there are, the more it tests whether you can avoid dropping any of them. Here's our simplified overview so you can see the shape of it:

<div class="cx-rubric-wrap">
<table class="cx-rubric">
<thead><tr><th>Row</th><th>✅ Excellent (4–5)</th><th>⚠️ Needs work (0–1)</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Abstract</td><td>Under 250 words; purpose, hypothesis, methods, results, conclusion — all clear and logically ordered</td><td>Too long or short; the five elements are vague or jumbled</td></tr>
<tr><td>Introduction</td><td>States the research problem, the purpose, and why it matters to the field</td><td>Problem or purpose never clearly stated</td></tr>
<tr><td>Methods</td><td>Materials, hypothesis, study design, statistics and steps all spelled out</td><td>Missing materials, no hypothesis, no statistics</td></tr>
<tr><td>Results</td><td>Data trends analyzed; tables and figures stand on their own</td><td>Trends not addressed; figures poor or missing</td></tr>

</div>

## 1. The abstract: five things inside 250 words

The rubric is blunt about it: a good abstract stays **under 250 words** and clearly covers **purpose, hypothesis, methods, results, and conclusion** — in an order that makes sense.

Two abstracts on roughly the same topic are below. **If you were the judge (0–5), what would you give each?** Think first, then open it up.

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Abstract A:</strong> "This study explores the effect of air pollution on human health. The results found the two are related, and further research is warranted."</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Abstract B:</strong> "To test whether commuter traffic affects street-level PM2.5, we measured a single road segment during morning and evening peaks for 14 days (methods), hypothesizing peak concentrations would be significantly higher (hypothesis). Peaks averaged 38% higher (results), with wind speed the strongest predictor (conclusion)."</div>

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see how a judge scores these</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Abstract A · about 1 point.</strong> Only half the elements are there — no hypothesis, no methods, and a conclusion that says nothing. The judge finishes reading and still doesn't know what you did.</p>
<p><strong>Abstract B · about 5 points.</strong> All five elements, and specific enough to be checked.</p>
<p>The judge's number is largely set within the 30 seconds it takes to read your abstract.</p>
</div>

## 2. Methods and results: does it hold up?

Under "Methods," the rubric looks for **all materials, the hypothesis or research questions, and the study design** — plus clear statistics if you used any. Under "Results," it looks for whether **data trends are analyzed** and whether the **figures can stand on their own**.

Keep one thing in mind: the judge is scoring whether your method can support your conclusion. How impressive the conclusion sounds is secondary.

What would you give each of these?

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Version A:</strong> "We ran the experiment and found A was better than B." (No control group, no repetitions, no figures; trends glossed over.)</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Version B:</strong> Variables and control group spelled out, repetitions and error stated; results shown in one figure that stands alone, noting "the difference only becomes significant after week 3."</div>

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see how a judge scores these</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Version A · about 1 point.</strong> With no control, no repetition and no figures, the judge has no way to tell whether the conclusion holds.</p>
<p><strong>Version B · about 5 points.</strong> Method and results can both be checked; even an ordinary conclusion scores well.</p>
</div>

## 3. Discussion: the students who name their limits score higher

This row separates projects more than any other. Tucked into the definition of "excellent" is a line many people miss: you must be able to **point out the limits of the research**, and speak to its practical or theoretical implications.

<div class="cx-note">Many students fear that admitting a weakness costs points, so they overstate the conclusion. To a judge, a student who writes an honest "limitations" section reads as far more mature than one who states everything as settled fact.</div>

## 4. Citations and formatting: the "boring" rows are scored too

The rubric gives **APA style and the bibliography** their own row: is the citation format right, and are the references **significant, published and relevant**? Grammar and spelling get a row as well — "professional tone, writing that flows" earns full marks, while "casual tone, hard to follow" sits at the bottom.

These look like small things, but they carry a message: **how seriously do you take your own research?**

## 5. The row that matters most in the age of AI: integrity

Two rows of the JSHS rubric are devoted to **integrity**: the **Statement of Outside Assistance (SOA)** and the **Acknowledgements**. The standard asks you to state honestly **who helped you and how**, signed by both the student and a teacher.

Why does this matter so much now? Because everyone writes with AI. Judges aren't afraid of you using tools — they're wary of a student who can't say clearly which parts were their own and which came from someone (or something) else.

<div class="cx-note">A student who states plainly "here's the tool I used, here's the advice my teacher gave" comes across as more credible, not less. Integrity is a place to gain points.</div>

## So the rubric is really a checklist for "what professional looks like"

Read the rows together and the sheet stops being mysterious. It keeps asking one question: **did you study something carefully and honestly, and can you explain it?**

- Abstract: are all five elements clear?
- Methods/results: does it hold up? Can the figures stand alone?
- Discussion: are you willing to name the limits?
- Format/citations: is it professional?
- Integrity: is it clear who helped with what?

When we mentor students, this is exactly the work — sitting with them and making every row on that sheet genuinely solid.

Every year, students finish a genuinely good health study and get cut at registration — not because the research was weak, but because **one form was signed too late**.

This piece covers the practical questions: which competition suits a medical or health project, what judges look for, and exactly what that disqualifying trap is.

## 1. First, which kind of study is yours?

This determines your paperwork — and whether you're at risk.

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Type A:</strong> Analyzing public datasets (CDC, WHO, NHANES), literature reviews, statistical modeling. <strong>No contact with real people.</strong></div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Type B:</strong> Surveys, interviews, measuring blood pressure or reaction time — <strong>anything involving human participants, even if they're your classmates.</strong></div>

**If you were the judge, which one do you think runs into trouble at registration?**

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see the answer</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Type B.</strong> The moment real people are involved, it counts as Human Participants Research, and the ISEF system requires <strong>IRB (Institutional Review Board) approval before any data collection begins</strong>.</p>
<p>Type A uses data that is already public and de-identified, so it usually doesn't need prior IRB review — which is exactly why many medical projects start from public databases.</p>
</div>
</details>

## 2. The trap that disqualifies students every year

Two students, and let's assume **the research quality is identical**. What happens to each?

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student A:</strong> Surveys 80 classmates over the summer, finishes the statistics, writes the paper. In the fall a teacher mentions IRB, so they submit for review, get approval, and register.</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Student B:</strong> Writes the research plan, submits it to the school IRB first. Only after the signed approval comes back do they start surveying.</div>

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see the result (this one stings)</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Student A · disqualified.</strong> The rule is explicit: the IRB review and signature date <strong>must come before any contact with participants</strong> — including recruitment and data collection. Getting it signed afterwards doesn't help; the date on the form tells the story. No amount of good research saves it.</p>
<p><strong>Student B · registers without issue.</strong> Exactly the right order.</p>
<p><strong>The key difference:</strong> it isn't <em>whether</em> you did the IRB, it's <strong>when</strong>. This is the most common — and most avoidable — disqualification each year, because it has nothing to do with the quality of the work.</p>
</div>
</details>

<div class="cx-note"><strong>Remember this one line:</strong> review first, then collect data. Get the order wrong and the whole study is wasted.</div>

**What is an IRB?** Less intimidating than it sounds. In the science-fair system, a school IRB needs at least three people: an educator (not your own adult sponsor), a school administrator (usually the principal or vice principal), and a medical or mental-health professional. Many schools already have one — ask your teacher.

**Are there exemptions?** Some, such as testing an invention or program you designed yourself, where feedback is only about the product, no personal data is collected, and there's no health risk. But **the IRB decides whether you're exempt, not you** — when in doubt, submit. It costs you nothing.

## 4. What judges watch for in health research specifically

Beyond the usual experimental design, health topics carry two extra checkpoints:

<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Version A:</strong> "This study proves that daily sugary drinks cause reduced attention in adolescents, and schools should ban their sale entirely."</div>
<div class="cx-quiz">📄 <strong>Version B:</strong> "Within this sample (n = 62), sugary-drink intake showed a moderate negative correlation with attention-test scores (r = -0.41). This was a cross-sectional design, so causation cannot be inferred; the sample came from a single school and may be confounded by factors such as sleep duration."</div>

<details class="cx-reveal">
<summary>Open to see how a judge scores these</summary>
<div class="cx-reveal-body">
<p><strong>Version A · about 1 point.</strong> Two serious problems: first, correlation stated as causation — the cardinal sin of health research; second, a jump straight to policy recommendations well beyond what the data supports. The word "proves" alone puts a judge on alert.</p>
<p><strong>Version B · about 5 points.</strong> Correct statistical language, the design's limits stated plainly, confounders acknowledged. The conclusion stays inside what the data can carry.</p>
<p><strong>The key difference:</strong> a high-schooler almost never has the resources to demonstrate causation in health research. <strong>The student willing to say "this is only a correlation" reads as more professional, not less.</strong></p>
</div>
</details>

## 5. A checklist before you start

- [ ] Does my study involve real people? (If yes → IRB first, always)
- [ ] Is the IRB approval date **earlier** than my first day of data collection?
- [ ] Are informed consent/assent forms signed by participants and parents?
- [ ] Is the data de-identified? Is participant privacy protected?
- [ ] Does my conclusion say "correlated with" or "causes"? Am I overstating?
- [ ] Have I honestly written out the study's limitations?
- [ ] Does my Statement of Outside Assistance say clearly who helped with what?

## Finally

Medical and health topics are among the most compelling in any science fair, because they sit so close to real life. And precisely because people are involved, the rules are the strictest.

The good news: the rules themselves aren't hard. What's hard is **knowing when each step has to happen**. A research plan submitted for review beforehand, versus a form signed after the fact — that's the difference between competing and not.
